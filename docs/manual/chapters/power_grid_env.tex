\chapter{Power Grid Frequency Control}\label{chap:power_grid}

\code{env\_lib.power\_grid\_env.PowerGridEnv} (registered as \envid{PowerGrid-v0},
new in version 1.1) is a benchmark for decentralised frequency control of a
networked power system. The $n$ buses of a transmission network, synchronous
generators or inverter-based resources, are the agents. They are coupled
through the power lines and obey the classical swing equations. Step load
changes and random load fluctuations push the bus frequencies away from their
nominal value and excite lightly damped electro-mechanical oscillations; every
agent injects a bounded, fast frequency response (for example from storage or
inverter headroom) to arrest the frequency drop and damp the oscillations,
using only local measurements and aggregates over its neighbourhood in the
network.

The environment is designed for research on networked multi-agent control: the
interaction graph and all physical parameters are explicit and reproducible,
the reward is a sum of per-agent terms, the benchmark system is fixed by a seed
while \code{randomize\_network=True} resamples topology and parameters for
robustness studies, and the dynamics are written once for a batch of grids, so
\code{PowerGridVectorEnv} simulates thousands of grids at once
(Section~\ref{sec:power-grid-vector}). The decentralised droop controller is
included as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:power-grid-baseline}). Typical uses are
learning decentralised controllers that outperform droop control (for example
by adapting gains to the local inertia or by exploiting neighbour information),
studying the effect of the network topology and of low inertia, and
comparing learned policies with model-based designs.

\section{Model}\label{sec:power-grid-model}

Bus $i$ has the voltage (or rotor) angle $\theta_i$ in radians and the frequency
deviation $\omega_i$ in rad/s from the nominal angular frequency
$\omega_0 = 2\pi f_0$ ($f_0$ = \code{nominal\_frequency}); the frequency deviation
in hertz is $\Delta f_i = \omega_i / 2\pi$. On a lossless, Kron-reduced network
(the load buses are eliminated, so every node is an active bus) with line
susceptances $B_{ij} \ge 0$ (per unit), the swing equations read
\begin{equation}
  \dot\theta_i = \omega_i, \qquad
  M_i \dot\omega_i = P_i(t) - D_i \omega_i
      - \sum_{j} B_{ij} \sin(\theta_i - \theta_j) + u_i,
  \label{eq:swing}
\end{equation}
with the inertia coefficient $M_i = 2H_i/\omega_0$ ($H_i$ the inertia constant
in seconds), the damping coefficient $D_i = d_i/\omega_0$ ($d_i$ the per-unit
damping, which lumps the frequency sensitivity of the loads and mechanical
damping), the net injection $P_i(t) = P_i^0 + \Delta P_i(t)$ and the control
injection $u_i \in [-u_{\max,i}, u_{\max,i}]$. The sum
$P^e_i = \sum_j B_{ij}\sin(\theta_i - \theta_j)$ is the electrical power exported
by bus $i$. Powers are in per unit of a common base, time in seconds.

\paragraph{Network and operating point.} The line graph is built with the
function \code{make\_graph} of \pkg{env\_lib.utils.graphs} (topologies in
Table~\ref{tab:power-grid-topologies}); $H_i$, $d_i$ and the susceptance of every
line are drawn uniformly from \code{inertia\_range}, \code{damping\_range} and
\code{susceptance\_range}. The operating point is constructed so that it is an
exact and stable equilibrium with a prescribed stress. A raw injection pattern
$z_i \sim \mathcal{U}(-1, 1)$, shifted to zero mean, is solved with the
linearised (DC) flow equations $L_B \theta^{\mathrm{DC}} = z$ ($L_B$ the
susceptance-weighted Laplacian, $\sum_i \theta^{\mathrm{DC}}_i = 0$); the angles
are scaled so that the most loaded line operates at the loading
$\ell$ = \code{line\_loading},
\begin{equation}
  \theta^\star = s\,\theta^{\mathrm{DC}}, \qquad
  s = \frac{\arcsin \ell}{\max_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{E}} |\theta^{\mathrm{DC}}_i - \theta^{\mathrm{DC}}_j|},
  \qquad
  P_i^0 = \sum_j B_{ij} \sin(\theta^\star_i - \theta^\star_j),
  \label{eq:power-grid-operating-point}
\end{equation}
and the nominal injections are the exact flows at these angles (they sum to
zero). Hence $(\theta^\star, 0)$ is a synchronous equilibrium of
\eqref{eq:swing} without disturbance and control, to machine precision. Since
every line angle satisfies $|\theta^\star_i - \theta^\star_j| < \pi/2$, the
linearisation couples the buses through a Laplacian with positive weights
$B_{ij}\cos(\theta^\star_i - \theta^\star_j)$, and the equilibrium is stable
(up to the rotational symmetry of the angles). A test verifies that Newton's
method on the flow equations, started from the DC solution, converges to the
same angles. Every episode starts from this equilibrium, so only the
disturbances drive the system away from it. The benchmark network is sampled
once at construction from \code{network\_seed} (default 0), so the default system
is the same for every user.

\paragraph{Disturbances.} The injection disturbance is
$\Delta P_i = S_i + X_i$. The step component $S_i$ receives step load changes:
at reset, \code{initial\_disturbances} distinct buses are hit; afterwards, at
every step boundary an event occurs with probability $1 - e^{-\lambda \Delta t}$
($\lambda$ = \code{disturbance\_rate}, $\Delta t$ = \code{dt}) at a uniformly random
bus. Step sizes $|\Delta P|$ are uniform in \code{disturbance\_magnitude}; a step
is a load increase (a negative change of the injection) with probability
\code{load\_increase\_prob}. Steps persist until the end of the episode. The
optional Ornstein--Uhlenbeck fluctuation $X_i$ has the stationary standard
deviation $\sigma$ = \code{noise\_std} and the correlation time
$\tau$ = \code{noise\_tau}; it starts from its stationary distribution and is
updated exactly at every step boundary,
\begin{equation}
  X_i(t_{k+1}) = a\, X_i(t_k) + \sigma \sqrt{1 - a^2}\, \xi_{i,k}, \qquad
  a = e^{-\Delta t / \tau}, \quad \xi_{i,k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\end{equation}
All random draws come from \code{self.np\_random}, in a vectorised way. The
disturbance sequence of a seeded episode does not depend on the actions, so
different controllers can be compared on identical disturbances.

\paragraph{Integration.} The action and the disturbance are held constant over
the control interval $\Delta t$ (zero-order hold). Equation \eqref{eq:swing} is
integrated with the classical fourth-order Runge--Kutta scheme and
\code{substeps} sub-steps of length $h = \Delta t/\code{substeps}$. The fastest
electro-mechanical mode is bounded by
$\omega_{\max}^2 \le \max_i 2\sum_j B_{ij}/M_i$ (Gershgorin); the environment
warns when $h\,\omega_{\max}$ exceeds 2.5, close to the stability limit of RK4 on
the imaginary axis. On the default system, after 5 seconds of an undisturbed
recovery from a step load change (largest $|\omega_i|$ 0.354 rad/s), the largest
deviation from a reference solution with 32 sub-steps was
$1.3\cdot10^{-3}$ rad/s with one sub-step, $8.7\cdot10^{-5}$ rad/s with two (the
default) and $5.5\cdot10^{-6}$ rad/s with four: the expected fourth-order
convergence.

\paragraph{Energy function.} Without disturbance and control, the classical
energy function of the swing equations,
\begin{equation}
  V = \tfrac12 \sum_i M_i \omega_i^2
      - \sum_i P_i^0 (\theta_i - \theta^\star_i)
      + \tfrac12 \sum_{i,j} B_{ij}
        \bigl(\cos(\theta^\star_i - \theta^\star_j) - \cos(\theta_i - \theta_j)\bigr),
\end{equation}
satisfies $\dot V = -\sum_i D_i \omega_i^2 \le 0$. The tests check that $V$
decreases monotonically in simulation and is conserved without damping.

\paragraph{The default system.} The default benchmark has 16 buses and 32 lines
(small-world graph, bus degrees 2 to 5), inertia constants between 2.5 and 7.6
seconds and nominal injections between $-0.62$ and $0.50$ per unit. Its
linearisation around $\theta^\star$ has 15 oscillatory modes between 0.55 and
2.41 Hz with damping ratios between 1.4\,\% and 4.4\,\%, and a
centre-of-inertia mode with the time constant $\sum_i M_i/\sum_i D_i = 3.1$
seconds. Without control a load step therefore produces a slow drift of the
system frequency with visible, slowly decaying inter-area oscillations; with
the droop baseline ($k = 0.25$) the damping ratios of the oscillatory modes
rise to between 0.35 and 0.95.

\section{Spaces}\label{sec:power-grid-spaces}

\paragraph{Action.} \code{Box(-u\_max, u\_max, (n,), float32)}: the control
injection $u_i$ of every bus in per unit (default bound 0.1). Any array with $n$
entries is accepted; values are clipped to $[-u_{\max,i}, u_{\max,i}]$, and NaN or
infinite values raise \code{ValueError}.

\paragraph{Observation.} \code{Box(-inf, inf, (n, 10), float32)}. Row $i$ holds
quantities a controller at bus $i$ can measure locally, aggregates over its
$\kappa$-hop neighbourhood $\mathcal{N}_i$ ($\kappa$ = \code{neighbourhood},
default 1, bus $i$ excluded) and two local parameters
(Table~\ref{tab:power-grid-obs}). Every feature is multiplied by a fixed factor
(\code{OBSERVATION\_SCALE}), chosen so that typical values are of order one; the
factors do not depend on the constructor arguments, so a policy can recover the
physical values. \code{env.observation\_layout} returns the column slice of
every key and \code{env.observation\_scale} the factors.

\section{Reward and episode end}\label{sec:power-grid-reward}

Agent $i$ is penalised for its frequency deviation, its rate of change of
frequency $\rho_i = (\omega_i(t_{k+1}) - \omega_i(t_k))/\Delta t$ and its control
effort, all evaluated at the end of the step,
\begin{equation}
  r_i = -\bigl(w_f\, \omega_i^2 + w_\rho\, \rho_i^2 + w_u\, u_i^2\bigr)\,\Delta t
        \;-\; c_{\mathrm{trip}}\, \mathbb{1}[\text{trip}],
  \label{eq:power-grid-reward}
\end{equation}
with $w_f$ = \code{w\_freq} (default 1), $w_\rho$ = \code{w\_rocof} (0.1),
$w_u$ = \code{w\_u} (10) and $c_{\mathrm{trip}}$ = \code{trip\_penalty} (100). The
team reward returned by \code{step()} is $R = \sum_i r_i$; the per-agent rewards
are in \code{info["agent\_rewards"]}. The weights were chosen so that all three
terms matter for a good controller: over 1000 default episodes with the droop
baseline, the three terms contributed on average 0.27, 0.31 and 0.17 to the
team cost of an episode (frequency, RoCoF, control), whereas without control
the frequency term dominates (68 on average) and with random actions the RoCoF
term does (330).

\code{terminated} is \code{True} when the largest deviation exceeds the trip
limit, $\max_i |\omega_i| > 2\pi f_{\lim}$ with $f_{\lim}$ =
\code{frequency\_limit} (default 0.5 Hz), which stands for loss of stability or a
protection trip; every agent then receives the extra penalty
$-c_{\mathrm{trip}}$. The default penalty exceeds the frequency cost of
operating at the limit for a whole default episode
($w_f (2\pi f_{\lim})^2 \cdot 10\,\mathrm{s} \approx 99$ per agent), so tripping
early never pays off through the frequency term. \code{truncated} is
\code{True} after \code{max\_steps} steps.

\section{Parameters}\label{sec:power-grid-params}

10 seconds of simulated time (200 steps of 50 ms, a realistic update period of
fast frequency response), a disturbance a sizeable fraction of a bus injection,
and the uncontrolled system stable but slow to settle.

\section{Baseline controller}\label{sec:power-grid-baseline}

\code{droop\_policy(obs, gain=0.25)} (module-level function in
\code{env\_lib.power\_grid\_env}) implements decentralised droop control, the
classical primary frequency response,
\begin{equation}
  u_i = \operatorname{clip}\bigl(-k\,\omega_i,\; -u_{\max,i},\; u_{\max,i}\bigr),
  \label{eq:droop}
\end{equation}
with the gain $k$ in per unit power per rad/s. It reads $\omega_i$ and
$u_{\max,i}$ from agent $i$'s own observation row and works for any leading batch
shape, \code{(..., n, 10) -> (..., n)}, so the same function drives the single
and the vector environment. \code{env\_lib.baseline\_policy(env)} returns it for
\envid{PowerGrid-v0}.

The droop gain is limited by the sampling of the controller. Over one control
interval, the local loop of bus $i$ behaves approximately like
$\omega_i(t_{k+1}) \approx (1 - k\Delta t / M_i)\,\omega_i(t_k)$, which is
non-oscillatory for $k\Delta t < M_i$ and unstable for $k\Delta t > 2M_i$ (the
control then chatters between its bounds). With the default ranges
($H_i \ge 2$ s at 50 Hz, $\Delta t = 0.05$ s) the default gain $0.25$ satisfies
the first condition for every admissible network. Table~\ref{tab:power-grid-baseline}
compares zero control, uniformly random actions and droop control with several
gains over 1000 default episodes; droop control improves the mean return by two
orders of magnitude and never trips. On the default network (smallest inertia
constant 2.5 s) the best uniform gain lies near $0.5$, while $k = 0.75$ violates
the sampling condition at the low-inertia buses: the RoCoF term explodes. A
learned policy can use the \code{inertia} and \code{rocof} features to go beyond
a uniform gain.

\section{Vector environment}\label{sec:power-grid-vector}

The native vector environment
\begin{pycode}
PowerGridVectorEnv(num_envs=1, *, autoreset_mode="next_step", render_mode=None, **kwargs)
\end{pycode}
accepts every keyword argument of \code{PowerGridEnv} and simulates
\code{num\_envs} grids as one batch; \code{env\_lib.make\_vec} returns it for
\envid{PowerGrid-v0}. Observations have shape \code{(num\_envs, n, 10)} and
actions \code{(num\_envs, n)}. The reward of a copy is its team reward, and
every info key of Table~\ref{tab:power-grid-info} is returned as a batched array;
for example, \code{infos["agent\_rewards"]} has shape \code{(num\_envs, n)}. The
info of a copy that was reset automatically within a call describes its new
episode, as in Gymnasium's \code{SyncVectorEnv}. The reset options accept
arrays of shape \code{(n,)} (broadcast to all copies) or \code{(num\_envs, n)},
together with \code{"reset\_mask"}. \code{render()} draws copy 0.

The dynamics are implemented once for a batch of grids (the single
environment is the batch of size one), with the state stored node-major and
the network coupling computed as
$P^e = \sin\theta \odot (B\cos\theta) - \cos\theta \odot (B\sin\theta)$ with one
sparse matrix product, so the cost per step grows with the number of lines
rather than with $n^2$. Copy 0 of the vector environment reproduces a single
environment started from the same state (injected with \code{reset(options=...)})
and driven by the same actions; the tests check this. With the default fixed
network all copies share it; with \code{randomize\_network=True} every copy owns
its network (a block-diagonal coupling matrix), resampled when the copy resets.

Table~\ref{tab:power-grid-throughput} reports the measured throughput with
constant actions (best of three runs on a 4-core x86-64 development machine
shared with other jobs), compared with Gymnasium's \code{SyncVectorEnv} of
single environments. A single environment step takes about 0.27 ms with 16 buses
and 0.38 ms with 128 buses (a reset about 0.2 ms).

\section{Rendering}\label{sec:power-grid-render}

The dashboard ($1000 \times 560$ pixels, Figure~\ref{fig:power-grid}) shows the
network on the left: buses coloured by their frequency deviation (diverging
colour map with a colour bar in hertz, a symmetric range that only grows within
an episode), a ring around every bus whose size shows its control effort
$|u_i|/u_{\max,i}$ (green when injecting, violet when absorbing power), lines
coloured and widened with their loading $|\sin(\theta_i - \theta_j)|$ and
triangles at buses with an active step load change (pointing down for a load
increase). On the right, the upper panel shows the frequency deviation of every
bus over time (thin traces), the centre-of-inertia frequency (bold), the
frequency nadir (dotted line and marker) and the trip limits over a shaded band
when they are within range; the lower panel shows the power balance, the net
control injection $\sum_i u_i$ against the load imbalance $-\sum_i \Delta P_i$ it
has to cover. The header reports the system size, step, time, largest deviation
and nadir, and a status badge: STABLE, ALERT (deviation above half the limit)
or TRIPPED. Both themes are supported. With blitting an \code{"rgb\_array"}
frame took about 20 ms (median) with 16 buses and 26 ms with 128 buses on the
development machine; the few frames per episode that rebuild the cached static
layer (when an axis range grows) took up to about 90 ms.

\section{Usage}\label{sec:power-grid-usage}

\begin{pycode}
import env_lib
from env_lib.power_grid_env import droop_policy

env = env_lib.make("PowerGrid-v0", render_mode="rgb_array")
obs, info = env.reset(seed=0)              # obs: (16, 10)
layout = env.unwrapped.observation_layout
episode_return = 0.0
while True:
    action = droop_policy(obs, gain=0.25)  # u_i = -k omega_i, clipped
    obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(action)
    episode_return += reward
    if terminated or truncated:
        break
print(info["frequency_nadir"], info["peak_abs_omega"], episode_return)
frame = env.render()                       # (560, 1000, 3) uint8
env.close()

# 1024 grids at once, each with its own random network
envs = env_lib.make_vec("PowerGrid-v0", 1024, randomize_network=True)
obs, infos = envs.reset(seed=0)            # obs: (1024, 16, 10)
obs, rewards, terminated, truncated, infos = envs.step(droop_policy(obs))
\end{pycode}

episodes and can record the droop controller:

\section{Background}\label{sec:power-grid-background}

The swing equation and the per-unit conventions follow the standard treatment
of power system stability and frequency control in textbooks such as Kundur's
\emph{Power System Stability and Control}. Network-reduced models with one
oscillator per generator go back to the classical transient-stability
literature; Bergen and Hill proposed the structure-preserving alternative, and
D\"orfler and Bullo analysed the Kron reduction of electrical networks and the
relation between power-network synchronisation and non-uniform Kuramoto
oscillators. The energy function of Section~\ref{sec:power-grid-model} is the classical
Lyapunov function of the direct methods of transient-stability analysis. The view of power
grids as networks of second-order phase oscillators was popularised in the
physics literature, for example by Filatrella, Nielsen and Pedersen, by Rohden,
Sorge, Timme and Witthaut and by Motter, Myers, Anghel and Nishikawa. Droop
control is the classical decentralised primary frequency control; distributed
averaging integral controllers, studied for instance by Simpson-Porco, D\"orfler
and Bullo and by Andreasson and co-authors, add secondary control that restores
the nominal frequency using neighbour communication, which the neighbour
features of the observation make possible. The default topology is the
small-world model of Watts and Strogatz.
